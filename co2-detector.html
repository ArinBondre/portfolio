CO₂ Detector

A compact indoor air quality monitor that turns sensor readings into clear visual and audible alerts, developed from a breadboard prototype into a portable, custom-built device.

Open one-page brief ↗

Abstract

A practical indoor air-quality indicator

Indoor air quality can affect health, concentration, and comfort. This project explores an accessible way to monitor air-quality-related readings using an Arduino-based embedded system and an MQ135 gas sensor. The device provides continuous readings and communicates threshold categories through three LEDs, with an acoustic warning at the critical level.

Custom PCB fabrication and a 3D-printed enclosure take the build beyond a temporary breadboard prototype, improving its durability, portability, and everyday usability. The project combines embedded programming, electronics, calibration, and maker-oriented fabrication in a compact environmental monitoring device.

01 · Motivation & objectives

Why build an air-quality detector?

Elevated indoor CO₂ can be an indicator of insufficient ventilation and limited air exchange. The goal was to build a simple device that makes changing conditions easy to notice, without requiring users to continuously interpret a numerical display.

  • Estimate air-quality-related CO₂ levels with an MQ135 sensor
  • Show acceptable, cautionary, and critical ranges with green, yellow, and red LEDs
  • Sound a buzzer when readings enter the critical range
  • Move from temporary wiring to a soldered PCB and custom 3D-printed housing

02 · Hardware & sensor integration

Accessible components, integrated into one system

The compact version is built around an Arduino Nano and an MQ135 air-quality sensor. Three LEDs provide at-a-glance status, and a buzzer supplies an audible warning. The sensor is connected to 5V, ground, and an analog input; the microcontroller reads its output and processes it with an MQ135 software library to produce a CO₂-related, ppm-style estimate.

  • Arduino microcontroller, with an Arduino Nano in the compact build
  • MQ135 sensor connected to power, ground, and an analog input
  • Three status LEDs and an acoustic buzzer
  • Custom-fabricated PCB and 3D-printed enclosure

03 · Software & alert logic

From sensor readings to clear feedback

The Arduino IDE was used to develop the firmware. Early tests displayed raw sensor readings in the serial monitor to verify the electronics. An MQ135 library was then used to convert sensor output into CO₂-related estimates for threshold-based alerts.

  • Below 1000 ppm: green LED indicates the acceptable range
  • 1000–2000 ppm: yellow LED indicates that ventilation should be considered
  • Above 2000 ppm: red LED and buzzer indicate the critical range

In the critical range, the buzzer is toggled in alternating cycles rather than held on continuously. Measurement updates run less frequently than buzzer updates, so the warning can remain responsive without reading the sensor on every buzzer toggle.

04 · Calibration & testing

Conditioning the sensor and checking the system

The MQ135 needs conditioning and calibration before its estimates can be interpreted. The documented process included an initial burn-in period of approximately 24–48 hours, followed by outdoor calibration using an assumed fresh-air baseline of about 400 ppm. The sensor's R0 resistance parameter was sampled 500 times and averaged for use as a calibration value in the program.

Testing progressed from confirming raw analog readings in the serial monitor, to checking the converted values, and then verifying that LED states and buzzer behavior changed with the programmed thresholds. This staged approach helped check both the sensor integration and the alert logic.

The MQ135 is a general air-quality sensor, not a dedicated, high-accuracy CO₂ instrument. Its ppm-style readings depend on calibration and environmental conditions, so this project is best understood as an experimental air-quality indicator rather than a certified measurement device.

05 · Prototype refinement

From breadboard to a portable build

The initial assembly used a larger Arduino board, breadboard, and jumper wires. That setup was useful for experiments, but it was bulky and susceptible to loose connections. The electronics were later migrated to a compact Arduino Nano and a soldered circuit board for a more stable, portable assembly.

The custom PCB layout was prepared with vector-based design tools. Copper paths were milled from copper-clad board, mounting and component holes were drilled, and components were soldered in place. A custom 3D-printed housing protects the electronics and keeps the detector compact enough to move between locations.

06 · Results & next steps

A working foundation, with room to improve

The completed prototype met its core goals: reading sensor output, signaling three threshold categories with LEDs, and sounding an alert in the critical range. The soldered PCB and custom enclosure improved the physical stability and portability compared with the original breadboard setup.

The main limitation is measurement precision: MQ135 readings vary with calibration and environmental conditions. Possible future improvements include a dedicated digital CO₂ sensor, a numerical display, battery power, long-term data logging, and wireless or smartphone connectivity.

The project is a useful proof of concept and a foundation for experimenting with more accurate sensing, data collection, and enclosure designs.
